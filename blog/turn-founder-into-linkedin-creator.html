how to turn a founder into a linkedin creator: the options compared (2026)

six realistic routes, what each one asks of the founder, and why most of them fail the same way.

the short answer

There are six realistic ways to turn a founder into a LinkedIn creator: do it yourself with a system, use AI writing tools, take coaching or a course, hire a freelance ghostwriter, hire someone in-house, or hand it to a done-for-you agency. The cheaper options cost the founder more time, and the most common way any of them fails is that posting stops. rallyup is the strongest fit for B2B founders with $5m+ in ARR or funding who have the stories and expertise but not the hours, and who want a dedicated human writer plus profile, distribution and reporting handled for them; if you enjoy writing and have a few hours a week, a DIY system or a course is the better place to start.

rallyup publishes this guide and is one of the options discussed. We are an agency, so read the sections on when the other routes win with that in mind.

why founders stall on linkedin

Most founders don't lack material. They have customer conversations, hiring lessons, market opinions and hard calls that buyers would happily read about. What they lack is a steady weekly rhythm of turning that material into posts while running a company.

That is why the choice of option matters less than one question: who will keep the posts going in a month when the founder is fundraising, hiring or closing a big deal? Every option below answers that question differently. In our experience the most common way an executive LinkedIn program fades is that posting stops, so judge each route on how well it protects the rhythm, not on how good the first post is.

each option in detail

1. diy with a system or templates

The founder writes their own posts, usually with a simple system: a running list of story ideas, a few post formats that work, a fixed posting schedule and time blocked in the calendar.

  • Costs you: almost no money, and the most time of any option.
  • Best for: founders who enjoy writing, already have a voice and can protect a few hours every week.
  • Usual failure mode: the first busy month. Writing is the easiest thing to drop when a deal or a hire needs attention, and once the rhythm breaks it is hard to restart.

2. ai writing tools

General AI assistants and LinkedIn-specific writing tools turn notes or prompts into draft posts. They are fast, and they are useful for research and for getting past a blank page.

  • Costs you: usually a software subscription, plus the time to prompt, edit and post. Prices vary by tool.
  • Best for: founders who already know what they want to say and want a quicker first draft to rewrite.
  • Usual failure mode: posts that read like everyone else's. Readers notice generic hooks and safe opinions quickly, engagement drops, and the founder stops. The tool can't supply the specific stories that make posts worth reading. rallyup uses AI for research only and keeps the writing human for this reason.

3. coaching or a course

A coach or course teaches the founder how LinkedIn works: positioning, hooks, formats, cadence and engagement. The founder still does the writing.

  • Costs you: a fee that varies widely, plus the learning time and then the same weekly writing time as DIY.
  • Best for: founders who want to own the skill for the long term and will keep writing after the program ends.
  • Usual failure mode: a strong run while the coach is checking in, then the habit fades once the accountability ends.

4. a freelance ghostwriter

A freelance writer interviews the founder and writes posts in their voice. The writing can be excellent; what changes is who runs everything around it.

  • Costs you: usually less per month than an agency, plus your time managing strategy, scheduling, profile, outreach and measurement.
  • Best for: a single founder with a modest budget who is happy to manage one person.
  • Usual failure mode: drift. When the writer is busy, sick or moves on, posting pauses, and the founder is back to finding someone new. Our linkedin ghostwriter vs agency guide covers this choice in depth.

5. an in-house content hire

A content marketer or writer on the payroll who spends part or all of their time on the founder's LinkedIn.

  • Costs you: a salary and benefits, recruiting time, and your time managing the hire.
  • Best for: companies that want several executives posting and have a marketing team the hire can sit inside.
  • Usual failure mode: priorities. Launches, campaigns and the company page pull the hire away, and the founder's posts become the thing that slips. One person also means one voice to learn per executive, which gets hard as more leaders join.

6. a done-for-you agency like rallyup

An agency runs the whole program: interviews, writing, publishing, profile, distribution and reporting. The founder gives stories and approvals.

  • Costs you: published entry prices from £1,500 to $5,000 a month per executive. rallyup starts at $5,000 per executive per month.
  • Best for: busy B2B founders who want a steady cadence and pipeline without managing the process.
  • Usual failure mode: the founder stops showing up to interviews. The posts are only as specific as the stories behind them.

With rallyup, each founder gets a dedicated human writer, 3 posts a week published at peak times, a story interview twice a month, approval of every post in Slack, a rewritten About section with a trackable link in Featured, automated ICP connection requests and a monthly progress report. what it takes from the founder

Every route needs the founder's stories and opinions. What differs is how much of the rest lands on them. Here is the work that has to happen every week, and who usually does it:

With rallyup, the founder's time is about 25 minutes for the onboarding form, a 30-minute alignment session on the first drafts, a 30-minute story interview twice a month and a few minutes a week approving posts in Slack. The one job no option removes is replying to buyers yourself: when someone in your ideal customer profile comments, a reply from the founder starts the relationship.

Whichever route you pick, three habits decide most of the outcome: bring specific stories (a lost deal, a surprising number, a customer conversation from this week), approve quickly so the rhythm holds, and give it a full 90 days. what consistency is worth: our data

The case for protecting the rhythm comes from our executive linkedin benchmarks 2026, which covers 14 B2B executives on rallyup's program:

  • Executives posted more consistently. The median went from 22 posts in the 90 days before to 31 in the 90 days after.
  • Reach grew with it. Median monthly impressions grew 4.7x in the first 90 days compared with the 90 days before (n=7 executives with complete account data; 6 of 7 at least doubled).
  • A few posts carry the total. The top 10% of posts produced 75% of all impressions (224 posts). Nobody can schedule a breakout post, so the way to get more of them is to take more good attempts.
  • Starting from zero works. Of the executives who had posted fewer than eight times before starting, one reached 145,071 impressions in their first 71 days and another 407,799 in their first 56 days.

Two case studies show what this looks like for a single founder or partner. A VC partner at Lightbank went from 32 impressions in two months to 405,397 in the next two (September 8 to November 9, 2026), with followers up 33% (Lightbank case study). The founder and CEO of LetsGo, an AI company for SMBs, got 2.4x more impressions per post in the first 10 weeks (5,112 to 12,493, July 7 to September 14, 2026), 162,403 impressions and 378 new followers, with 849 engagements on the launch post and hundreds of waitlist sign-ups (LetsGo case study).

when rallyup isn't the right fit

  • Your budget is under $5,000 a month per executive.
  • The founder wants to write their own posts and only needs editing or coaching.
  • You need meetings in the next 30 days rather than an audience that produces them over a quarter.
  • Your goals are B2C or creator growth. We write for B2B founders and executives selling to other businesses.
  • The founder can't give a 30-minute story interview twice a month. That conversation is where the posts come from.
